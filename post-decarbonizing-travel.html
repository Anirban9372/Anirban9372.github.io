For Celonis Summer Analytics 2026, I got a dataset most people never see: 65,289 real corporate business trips, logged event-by-event, tied to a company's actual Net Zero by 2030 target. The brief was simple to state and hard to do well — find where the emissions actually come from, and turn that into recommendations someone could act on this quarter, not someday.

What the process data actually showed

Process mining on the trip event log surfaced three things that weren't obvious from the summary numbers alone:

  • Premium cabins are only 24.5% of all trips but drive 44.7% of total emissions — by far the single largest lever in the whole dataset.
  • APAC-origin trips average 3,824 kg CO₂e per trip versus 630 kg for EMEA — a 6× gap, driven structurally by long-haul dependency, not by any one department's behavior.
  • Out-of-policy trips ran 43% hotter than compliant ones (3,625 kg vs 2,525 kg average) — policy exceptions weren't just a compliance issue, they were a real carbon cost.

Building the dashboard

I built this out as a 4-tab Celonis dashboard — Emissions Overview, Benchmarking, Emissions Drilldown, and a Process Explorer — with 12+ components covering all five key business questions the brief required. The hardest part wasn't the charts; it was deciding which comparison actually answered "so what do we do about it," rather than just describing the data.

Three recommendations, each quantified

R1: Restrict Business Class on long-haul routes
~12,635 t CO₂e/yr · ~$632K/yr
→
R2: Tighten out-of-policy exception approvals
~3,960 t CO₂e/yr · ~$198K/yr
→
R3: Mandate train on Berlin–Madrid
~770 t CO₂e/yr · ~$38.5K/yr

Combined across a 3-year projection: roughly 79,800 tonnes of CO₂e avoided, around $3.9M in avoided carbon cost, and a 14.5% cut to the annual footprint — without touching a single low-emission trip.

What I actually learned

The biggest lesson wasn't technical — it was that "the problem is geography, not department." Every business unit clustered within a narrow 2,600–2,860 kg/trip range; no single team was the outlier. It would have been easy to build a dashboard that pointed fingers at whoever traveled most. The real insight only showed up once I stopped slicing by department and started slicing by route and cabin class instead. That reframing — asking a better question of the same data — mattered more than any chart I built.
